# Recording script — for the Professional Voice Clone

You need **17.4 more minutes** to reach ElevenLabs' 30-minute minimum. This is
that material, written so none of it is throwaway: the long reads work as show
openers, and the short ones join the drop rotation.

Aim for **20 minutes**, not 17.4. Some takes get discarded, and you want margin
over the minimum rather than landing exactly on it.

## Before you press record

The clone learns your recording setup as thoroughly as it learns your voice, so
this session decides what the voice sounds like forever.

- **A hand-span from the mic, angled slightly off-axis.** This is the fix for
  the breath you disliked — at close range a microphone hears every inhale.
- **One session, one room, one position.** Consistency matters more than any
  single take being perfect. If you move the mic halfway through, the clone
  learns an average of two setups.
- **Read at your natural pace.** Don't perform. The clone reproduces what it
  hears, including effort.
- **Leave the mistakes in.** Stop, breathe, say the line again. Trimming is my
  job — a restarted sentence costs nothing.
- **Don't process anything.** No noise reduction, no EQ, no filters in your
  recording app. Raw is what a cloner wants.

Save everything into `Audio_recorded/` alongside the first 56. Then:

```bash
python3 scripts/build_voice_dataset.py --all --merge --out sources/voice-training-pvc
```

# Part one — long reads

Continuous speech trains a clone better than short clips, so these matter most.
Read each straight through. Roughly nine minutes total.

## 1. What the station is

Pomato Radio, one-thirty-point-three. It's a small station, and it plays music
for the hour you're actually in rather than the same thing all day. Mornings are
soft and slow, because nothing has happened yet and there's no sense pretending
otherwise. By late morning it picks up a little. Lunch is brighter. The long
afternoon is where the day gets expensive — attention costs more at three
o'clock than it did at nine — so that stretch is deliberately minimal, long
tracks, low drama, nothing that demands anything from you.

Then the drive home, which is steady and calm and never in a hurry. The evening
has some energy in it. And the small hours are sparse, almost ambient, for
anyone still awake and not especially wanting company but not wanting silence
either.

The weekend runs differently. From Friday evening it stops being about the clock
and starts being about the sound. Afrobeats all Friday night, straight through
to Saturday morning. Soul and rhythm and blues to ease into Saturday, hip hop
through the afternoon, something harder as the night gathers, and house until
the sun comes up. Sunday is folk and acoustic and country, and it lands softly,
because Monday is coming whether any of us are ready for it.

That's the whole idea. One station, fourteen shows, and a person somewhere
behind it choosing what plays.

## 2. About the music

Everything on this station is here because someone said yes. Some of it I made.
Some of it comes from musicians who release their work freely and asked only to
be credited. None of it is here by accident, and none of it is here without
permission, which matters more to me than having every song you might expect.

That means you'll hear things you don't recognise. I think that's the point. A
station that only plays what's already popular isn't really choosing anything —
it's just agreeing with everyone else. I'd rather play you something you've
never heard and have you remember where you heard it.

If you make music and you'd like it on the air, the door is open. Get in touch,
tell me what you've made, and if it fits an hour I'll play it and I'll say your
name when I do.

## 3. On listening

There's a particular kind of listening that radio is good at and almost nothing
else does any more. You don't choose the next song. You don't skip. You let
somebody else decide, and you get on with whatever you were doing.

It sounds like a limitation. I think it's the whole appeal. Choosing is work,
and by the middle of the afternoon most of us have done enough of it. Handing
that over to a station — even a small one, even mine — is a small relief.

So I'll keep choosing, and you keep doing whatever it is you're doing, and every
couple of hours I'll say something brief and get out of the way again.

## 4. A longer sign-off

That's about it from me. Thank you for listening tonight — genuinely, thank you.
It's a strange thing, talking into a microphone for people you'll never meet,
and knowing somebody out there has this on while they work or drive or lie awake
is the entire reason to do it.

The music carries on after I stop talking. It always does. I'll be back in a few
hours, or in the morning, depending on where you are and when you're hearing
this. Look after yourself, get some sleep if you can, and I'll see you when I
see you.

Goodnight from Pomato Radio, one-thirty-point-three.

# Part two — show openers

One per show, longer than a drop, usable as a proper opener. Roughly five
minutes total.

**First Light** — Good morning, and welcome to First Light on Pomato Radio,
one-thirty-point-three. This is the softest the station gets. Nothing here is
going to demand anything from you — no big choruses, no surprises. Just
something to have on while the day works out what it wants to be.

**Slow Roll** — Good morning, this is Slow Roll. Late enough that you've eaten,
early enough that it still counts as morning. Warmer than the last couple of
hours, a bit more going on, still absolutely no rush.

**Midday** — Good afternoon, and welcome to Midday. Brighter now, and easy —
present without getting in the way. Whatever you're in the middle of, this is
music to be in the middle of it with.

**The Long Afternoon** — Good afternoon. This is The Long Afternoon, and it's
the hardest stretch of the day for most people. Attention is a resource and
you've been spending it since this morning. So this is minimal, and the tracks
are long, and nothing is going to jolt you.

**Slow Drive** — Good evening, and welcome to Slow Drive. If you're heading
home, this hour is for you. Steady, calm, nothing that makes anybody drive
faster than they should. Take your time. Get there in one piece.

**Night Garage** — Good evening, this is Night Garage. Deeper now, more groove
in it, house and garage as the evening properly arrives. The day's behind you
and the night hasn't decided what it is yet.

**Small Hours** — Good evening, or good morning — I've stopped keeping track.
This is Small Hours. Sparse, ambient, long stretches with almost nothing in
them. If you're still up, you're in good company, and I'm not going to ask why.

**Afro Friday** — Good evening, and welcome to Afro Friday on Pomato Radio. The
week is finished. This is afrobeats from now until sunrise, and there is no
plan beyond that.

**Saturday Soul** — Good morning, and welcome to Saturday Soul. Rhythm and
blues to ease you into the weekend, because nobody should be doing anything
strenuous before midday on a Saturday.

**Hip Hop Hours** — Good afternoon, and welcome to the Hip Hop Hours. Confident,
head-nod tempo, all the way through the afternoon.

**Trap** — Good evening. Harder from here. The night's getting its shoes on and
so are you.

**House Hours** — Good evening, and welcome to House Hours — the peak of the
weekend on Pomato Radio. We're going straight through to sunrise, so settle in
or don't, but stay with me either way.

**Sunday Folk** — Good morning, and welcome to Sunday Folk. Acoustic, gentle,
warm. There is nothing you need to do this morning that can't wait an hour.

**Country** — Good afternoon, and welcome to the country hour. Windows open,
feet up, and the last easy stretch before the week starts again.

# Part four — house promos and longer reads

These close the gap to thirty minutes, and the promos solve a problem you
already have: your "we'll be right back" drops need something to break *to*.
Roughly seven minutes.

## House promos

Ten to twenty seconds each. Read them like you mean them, not like adverts.

**1.** Pomato Radio is a one-person station. No playlist algorithm, no
committee, no meetings about what the audience wants. Just somebody choosing
records for the hour you're in. If that's the kind of thing you like, you're
already in the right place.

**2.** Every track on this station is played with permission. That's rarer than
it should be, and it means you'll sometimes hear a name you don't know. Stay
with it — that's usually the good part.

**3.** If you make music, Pomato Radio would like to hear it. Small station,
real listeners, and I'll say your name on air when I play you. Get in touch.

**4.** Fourteen shows, seven days, one station. Mornings soft, afternoons
minimal, Friday nights loud, Sundays gentle. One-thirty-point-three.

**5.** Nothing on this station repeats itself much, and nothing here is chosen
because it's popular. It's chosen because it fits the hour. That's the only
rule.

**6.** Pomato Radio, one-thirty-point-three. Tune in, feel good. That's the
whole promise, and I think we keep it.

## 5. Why the hours matter

Most stations play the same music at nine in the morning as they do at nine at
night, and I've never understood it. You are not the same person at those two
times. Nobody is.

At nine in the morning you're deciding what the day is going to be. The wrong
music at that hour is genuinely intrusive — too much energy, too many words,
somebody shouting about a competition. What you want is something that leaves
room. So that's what plays.

By the middle of the afternoon something different is happening. The research on
this is unambiguous — attention is a resource and it depletes, and there's a
measurable dip in the early afternoon that has nothing to do with willpower or
character. Fighting it with loud music doesn't work. What works is music that
doesn't ask for anything: long tracks, few changes, nothing that pulls your
head up.

Then in the evening the demand drops away and there's room for something with
more in it. And overnight, for the people still awake, something sparse enough
to sit with without becoming company you didn't ask for.

None of this is complicated. It's just paying attention to the fact that a day
has a shape, and letting the station have the same shape.

## 6. On small stations

There are stations with more listeners than this one. Most of them, in fact.
That used to bother me and it doesn't any more.

A big station has to please a lot of people at once, and the only reliable way
to do that is to play things almost nobody actively dislikes. That's not the
same as playing things people love. It produces a kind of smooth, agreeable
nothing — music as furniture.

A small station can do the opposite. It can play one strange record at two in
the morning because it's the right record for two in the morning, and if only
eleven people hear it, all eleven of them will remember it. I would rather have
that.

So this is a small station on purpose. It plays music somebody chose, for people
who wanted somebody to choose. If you've found your way here and stayed, you're
part of a fairly short list, and I don't take it lightly.

## 7. A morning read

Good morning. It's early, and I'm going to assume you didn't want to be awake
this early, because most people don't.

Here's what I know about mornings. The first hour is the only part of the day
nobody has claimed yet. Once you're properly into it, the day starts making
demands — messages, other people's priorities, things that were urgent before
you woke up. But right now, none of that has arrived. This hour is yours.

So don't spend it on anything that doesn't matter. Don't open the thing you know
you're going to open. Have the coffee, look out of the window, let the music do
what it's doing. The rest of it will still be there in an hour, and you'll be
better equipped for it.

That's my entire morning philosophy and it took me about fifteen years to work
out. You're welcome to it.

## 8. A late-night read

It's late. I don't know how late, because this was recorded at some other time
entirely, and by the time you hear it I'll be asleep. That's the strange thing
about radio at this hour — it's a conversation where one person left early.

Small Hours runs for seven hours, which is longer than any other show on the
station, and it's the one I think about most. Seven hours is a long time to fill
with music that has to be quiet without being boring, present without being
company. Most of it is ambient. Some of it barely qualifies as music at all —
long tones, field recordings, a piano with a lot of room around it.

The people who listen at this hour are a specific group. Some of you are
working. Some of you are driving somewhere you'd rather not be driving. Some of
you can't sleep and have given up trying for a while. And some of you have just
left something — a shift, a party, a conversation — and aren't ready for silence
yet.

I'm not going to pretend I know which one you are. That's the point of not
asking. What I can do is keep the music going and not talk very much, which is
what this hour asks for.

There's a particular quality to being awake when most people aren't. Everything
is easier to think about. Nobody's going to interrupt, nothing new is going to
arrive, and the problems that seemed urgent at four in the afternoon have gone
quiet. It's not that they're solved. They're just not shouting.

If you're using this hour for that, good. It's a decent use of it.

And if you're just tired and putting off going to bed — which, let's be honest,
is most of us most of the time — that's fine too. The music will keep going
either way. Stay as long as you like.

Whenever you do go, sleep well. I'll be here tomorrow, and the day after that,
saying more or less this same thing to whoever's still awake.

Goodnight from Pomato Radio, one-thirty-point-three.
